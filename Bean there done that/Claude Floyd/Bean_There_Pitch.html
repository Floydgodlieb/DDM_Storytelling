The brief·CRISP-DM

“Are we ready to grow as of next year?”

In January 2023 the company stopped accepting new customers to create a steady state. That makes 2023 a clean test: with demand frozen, can the operation run well? If not, growth will only make the problems larger.

We tested every step of the chain against one standard: could it absorb 20% more volume next year without losing coffee, customers or money?

Speaker A (60 s). At the start of 2023 the company froze its customer base. For us as analysts that is a gift: it is a controlled experiment. Demand was frozen, so if the operation struggled in 2023, it will struggle more with growth.

Roadblock 2 of 4Capacity

Both roasters already run a full shift, every working day

The packing line, shared by both roasters, also needs about 8.3 hours a day. There is no idle machine to absorb growth in the peak.

With 20% growth, autumn demand would exceed the current shift by about half. That is the capacity wall.

Hours of work per day: roasting (setup + run) per machine and packing
Source: Roasting. Hours = batches per day × (setup + run time). Packing = batches × packing time, both machines. Dashed line: one 8-hour shift.

Speaker B (60 s). Why not simply roast more in the autumn? Because the machines are full. We calculated how many hours each roaster works per day: batches times setup plus roasting time. The P60 runs 8.0 hours, the Px120 8.5 hours. The packing line, which both share, is also at about 8.3 hours. That is one complete shift on every machine, every working day.

So this is the wall. If the company grows 20 percent, autumn demand would be about 50 percent above what one shift can produce. Hand over to [C].

OpportunityCapacity hidden in the data

Before buying a third roaster, win back the lost minutes

Achieved vs. nameplate throughput (kg green coffee per roasting hour)

The Px120 delivers only 61% of its rated speed. 27% of every cycle is setup, not roasting. Halving setup time (SMED, campaign scheduling) adds about 15% capacity at almost no cost.

Rejected batches by roast and QC reason, 2023

Light roast fails QC 3.6× as often as the others (5.0% of batches), spread across weight, colour and profile. That points to the roast recipe on the Px120, not to one bad month.

Source: Roasting. Nameplate from case brief: P60 215 kg/h, Px120 480 kg/h.

Speaker C (75 s). There is good news in the roasting data. The Px120 is rated at 480 kilos per hour, but it achieves 292. Part of that is physics, but 27 percent of each cycle is setup time: six minutes between batches. Halving setup with quick-changeover methods and by roasting the same roast in longer campaigns gives about 15 percent extra capacity for almost no investment.

Second: light roast. One in twenty light roast batches is rejected, more than 3.5 times as often as medium or dark. The rejects are spread over weight, colour and profile, so this is a recipe and calibration problem, not bad luck. Fixing it saves 8 tonnes a year and machine time.

Roadblock 3 of 4Sourcing

Some months, one late container would stop a roaster

10 daysof green beans left in total at the end of July. Uganda was down to 1,226 kg.

Beans come by sea from Costa Rica, Uganda and Colombia, in lots of two containers (20,160 kg). Shipping from origin takes weeks. Orders follow no visible reorder point: stock swings from 10 to 50 days.

With 20% growth, the same buying pattern runs dry. Direct trade with three single farms is great for the story and fragile for supply.

Green bean stock per origin, in days of usage (month end)
Source: Inventory (raw material). Days = stock ÷ average monthly usage of that origin, implied from stock movements. Dashed: proposed 6-week safety level.

Speaker C (60 s). Further upstream: the green beans. We converted bean stock into days of usage. The dashed line is a six-week safety level, which is reasonable for coffee that travels by sea. Almost every origin dips below it, and at the end of July the company had beans for only 10 days in total. Uganda was down to 1,200 kilos.

There is no visible reorder rule: two containers arrive when someone orders them. In a steady state you get away with it. With growth, one delayed ship stops a roaster.

Roadblock 4 of 4The doorstep

Every parcel loses money, and some customers pay for a bug

Shipping result per order-value band: fee charged − PostNL cost (€)

We pay PostNL €6.75 per parcel and charge €4.95 below €55, nothing above. Net: −€1.02M a year, 5.8% of web revenue.

The free-shipping bug

We promise free shipping above €55. The checkout gives it from 5 bags. So 30,725 orders of three or four 1 kg bags (€69–€106) paid €152,089 they should not have.

Parcels delivered per weekday

92.1% of parcels arrive on the promised next working day, so 18,991 do not. Weekend orders pile up: Monday handles 2.5× a normal day.

Speaker C (75 s). The last roadblock is at the customer's door. Every order band loses money on shipping. PostNL costs 6.75 euro per parcel, we charge 4.95 below 55 euro and nothing above. In total that is minus 1 million euro a year.

We also found a bug. The website promises free shipping above 55 euro, but the checkout actually gives it from five bags. Someone ordering three 1-kilo bags for 69 euro still pays shipping. That happened 30,725 times: 152 thousand euro charged against our own promise. That is a quick fix and a reputation risk.

And delivery: 92 percent arrive on the promised day. Eight percent are late, spread evenly over the year, which points at the carrier agreement. Mondays get two and a half times the normal volume because nothing ships at the weekend.

Speaker A (45 s). So, the evaluation. Four roadblocks, each with evidence from 2023 and with what would happen if we grew 20 percent without changes. The most serious is capacity combined with planning: the company would lose about 55 tonnes of sales in the autumn, while still throwing coffee away in summer. None of these needs a third roaster or a new warehouse. They need planning, process discipline and a few hours more in the peak.

Recommendations·in this order

Six moves: fix, free, secure, then grow

Fix · Q1

Roast to demand

Monthly sales & operations planning using last year's season curve. Roast to a maximum of 4 weeks of stock, first in first out. Buy beans ahead for the peak, not roasted coffee.

KPI: expired kg → 0 · stock cover ≤ 28 days
Fix · Q1

Honour our own promise

Correct the checkout to free shipping from €55 order value. Renegotiate the PostNL rate and a 97% next-day SLA; add Saturday dispatch to flatten Mondays.

KPI: on-time ≥ 97% · shipping loss ≤ 3% of revenue
Free · Q1–Q2

Win back lost minutes

Halve Px120 setup time (quick changeover, campaign scheduling per roast). Recalibrate the light roast profile and colour targets.

KPI: setup ≤ 3 min · light reject ≤ 1.5% · +15% capacity
Free · Q2–Q3

A peak shift, Sep–Feb

Recruit and train a second roasting and packing shift for the six peak months, ready by September. Flexible contracts, no new machines.

KPI: peak utilisation ≤ 90% of available hours
Secure · Q1

Reorder points for beans

6-week safety stock per origin, reorder point per farm, single containers more often instead of double lots. Add a back-up supplier per origin.

KPI: green cover ≥ 42 days, every origin, every month
Grow · from Q2

Reopen, smartly

Reopen for new customers in spring, when capacity is free. Target offices and the Randstad first: flat demand, cheap delivery, the truck already goes there.

KPI: +20% volume · B2B share 2% → 8%

Speaker A (90 s). Our advice is six moves, in a fixed order. First fix: roast to demand with a monthly planning meeting, maximum four weeks of stock; and honour our own promise by fixing the shipping bug and renegotiating with PostNL. Second, free capacity: halve the setup time and fix light roast quality, then add a second shift only in the six peak months. Third, secure the beans with reorder points and safety stock. Only then grow: reopen in spring, starting with offices and the Randstad.

Every move has a KPI, so the CEO can check progress every month. Let us show you what this does in numbers.

Appendix B·method & data quality

Assumptions and what the data cannot tell us

Assumptions

  • Bag type 1 = 1 kg, bag type 2 = 500 g (confirmed by price per bag: €22.98 vs €11.49 for dark roast).
  • Delivery promise = next working day, excluding Dutch public holidays.
  • Roasting hours = batches × (setup + run time); cooling overlaps with the next batch.
  • Expired value at shelf price per kg from the finished-goods sheet; green bean cost uses the weighted purchase price (€8.32/kg) and 16.9% roast loss.
  • Population per province approximated from CBS (1 Jan 2023).
  • Simulator: 1 shift = 2023 monthly output; extra shift = 90% as productive; 30-day freshness window.

Data quality findings

  • Packaging stock does not reconcile. We sell about 83,000 bags of 500 g a month, but the bag stock drops by only about 20,000 a month. The bag inventory is not tracked reliably, so stock-outs could come as a surprise.
  • Shipping boxes do reconcile: about 20,000 used a month, one per web order.
  • Orders have a date but no time. Same-day dispatch cut-offs cannot be tested.
  • The data has no cost price, wages or energy, so we report value at shelf price and green-bean cost rather than profit.
  • Which origin goes into which roast is unknown, so bean risk is shown per origin.

Recommendation: one inventory system that scans packaging use at the packing line, and a cost price per roast.
